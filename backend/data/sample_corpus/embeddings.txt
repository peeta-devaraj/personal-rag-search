Word and sentence embeddings map text into dense, low-dimensional vectors such that semantically similar pieces of text end up close together in the vector space, typically measured by cosine similarity. Unlike sparse representations such as one-hot vectors or TF-IDF, which treat every term as an independent dimension, dense embeddings are learned so that they capture distributional and contextual meaning, allowing a system to recognize that "car" and "automobile" are related even though they share no characters.

Sentence embedding models used for retrieval are often trained with a contrastive objective, where the model is pushed to place a query and its relevant passage close together while pushing unrelated passages further apart. Some retrieval-oriented embedding models are asymmetric: they use a different prefix or encoding path for queries than for documents, since a short question and a long passage that answers it are not simply paraphrases of each other.
